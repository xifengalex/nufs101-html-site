Why does meat change color?

Meat color is chemistry. It depends on one protein, myoglobin, and on what its iron atom is bound to. Explore how oxygen, curing and cooking change it.

1. Heme pigments

Both pigments hold iron inside a porphyrin ring (the same ring shape as chlorophyll, but with Fe instead of Mg).

The whole protein: globin folds around the heme

Myoglobin is a single chain of helices (ribbons) that wraps around one heme group (the sticks near the center). The heme's iron is the only part that binds oxygen, and the surrounding globin keeps the iron in the right state.

Limited oxygen produces metmyoglobin.

With no oxygen, myoglobin stays purple-red. With plenty of oxygen it becomes oxymyoglobin (cherry red). But at a low, limited oxygen level (around 1–2% O₂), iron is oxidized to Fe³⁺ most easily, so metmyoglobin builds up and the meat turns brown. Try the "Very little" button.

3. Color layers in a whole muscle

This is why a freshly cut steak looks purple inside and "blooms" cherry red after about 15–30 minutes in air. It is also why meat that has been stored too long turns brown, which is less acceptable to shoppers even though browning alone does not mean spoilage.

4. Cured meat pigments

Curing adds nitrite, which becomes nitric oxide (NO). NO binds to the iron in myoglobin and gives cured meats like ham their stable pink color. Step through it.

Oxymyoglobin

Why it matters: cured pigments resist oxidation, so cured meat stays pink even when cooked. Pink in cured meat is not a sign it is undercooked.

5. Safe cooking temperatures

Color is not a reliable doneness test: meat can brown before it is safe, and cured or well-oxygenated meat can stay pink after it is safe. Use a food thermometer in the thickest part.
